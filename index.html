Prosthetic Finger Research

Read the submitted conference paper here

My final year undergrad engineering project was on the research and development of novel humanlike prosthetics; with the help of my supervisor I was also to turn this research into a conference paper submission. This was certainly not a solo endeavor, and I was lucky enough to work with my partner, Raymond Wallace, and our supervisor, Minas Liarokapis.

Exploded CAD View of the Prosthetic Finger

The fundamental goal of humanlike transradial prosthetics (i.e. below the elbow) is to restore human functionality to those suffering from upper body limb loss. Unfortunately, current prosthetics are unable to do so, requiring constant tradeoffs between cost, functionality, weight, durability, and how "humanlike" they are. To oversimplify the field quite a bit, two basic paradigms have arisen for practical prosthetics: 1) Highly complicated biomechatronic devices equipped with motors and sensor interfaces for high functionality, but also very high cost, high weight, low durability, and 2) Low cost, body powered devices usually using a shoulder harness for actuation, which take advantage of underactuation and have relatively low functionality, but are inexpensive, lightweight, robust, and durable.

My work is within the mechanical transmission mechanism of prosthetics, specifically using a roller chain rather than a tendon for increased robustness and longevity, making the design well suited for prosthetics of the second type. The design itself comprises only a single humanlike finger (for now ;) and utilizes only a single actuator in order to minimize control burden and exhibit underactuated, adaptive grasping capabilities. This essentially means that on closure the finger(s) will conform to the shape of the grasped object, exerting force to the joint with the least resistance, resulting in stable grasps. Please feel free to read the submitted conference paper for more details.

Dynamic Pathfinding Robots

See the source code and more details here

This pair of projects was completed for a final year Mechatronics course, where I and three other students were tasked with designing and building a small arduino-based robot. This involved CAD design and 3D printing, a little breadboard electronics, and ~2000 lines of C++ code to implement the algorithms and closed loop control systems necessary for the robot to operate and adapt to a real time environment with moving obstacles.

Frontal Sensor Array

Project 1

The first project required our robot to cover every square in a grid, as observed by an overhead computer vision system, after being placed randomly in an X by Y walled area. The scenario given was that the robot was intended to fertilize a field, and needed to cover the entire area in an even pattern.

The primary difficulty came from implementing control logic to overcome faulty hardware. Both the motors and sensors we were requierd to use for the project were (intentionally?) extremely unreliable; the motors would provide wildly varying torque and speed outputs for the same voltage input, and the sensors would give a high percentage of faulty outputs, including . We eventually overcame these issues by implementing a PID controller for each motor, along with several automatic scaling and correction factors to allow the robot to drive straight. The sensors required a couple of signal processing algorithms, including comparisons to previous and expected outputs, in order to produce useable data and guide the robot in the correct direction.

Project 2

The second project was more complicated, and required us to build upon the learnings of the first. Instead of a static environment, the robot was placed into an environment with moving obstacles, and tasked with finding and approaching several light sources in order to blow on them with a small fan, completed in the least time possible. The scenario was an automated firefighting robot in an active disaster zone.

This project required all of the same control systems as the first project, with additional logic to allow the robot to scan the environment, detect the light sources, and travel towards them, dynamically avoiding obstacles. This required several control patterns, implemented using a finite state control system, which enabled the robot to prioritize the correct action in a given situation. The first issue we had to overcome was the physical design of the robot, in order to efficiently scan the environment and avoid obstacles. We found that a partially rotating sensor array paired with the smallest frame practical gave us the best results. The second issue we overcame was on the control side, developing our algorithms in such a way as to allow the robot to break out of edge cases, such as when an obstacle would move in front of the robot such that it seemed to be blocked on all sides, or when the robot would continuously gain and lose sight of the target lights. We eventually solved this through rearchitecting the control flow to limit the number of executions of each state logic before attempting an alternative action.
